Best Sites — What To Pick, And Why

Phase 2 · The Rest of DevOps

What this is and why it matters

This page is a map: the best free places to learn each topic, and the paid ones worth paying for later.

Think of a road map for a long trip. The map does not teach you to drive. It shows you the whole road, in order, and which places to stop at for each part of the journey.

In real learning, the failure is paying for a big course before you know what you need — and never finishing it.

Start free. Pay only when you know what you need, and let your own projects do the proving.

Reference tool
This page supports every topic — use it whenever you are stuck. It has no ladder of its own.
Try it Yourself »Open the practice terminal and run it yourself — nothing installs. Included with Phase 2. Simulation — practice version. The real thing is opening the sites yourself.

Words you need, before you use them

  • A map is a picture of the whole path: what to learn, and in what order.
  • A course is a set of lessons that teaches one topic, in order, with exercises.
  • A tutorial is a short guide that teaches one small thing, step by step.
  • Free means you pay nothing to use it.
  • Paid means you pay money. Some paid sites charge once per course. Some charge every month.
  • Official docs means the guide written by the people who made the tool. It matches the current version, because the makers update it when the tool changes.
  • A link is a web address. Click it, or type it into your browser's address bar, and the page opens. If a link fails (the page does not open), search for the site's name in a search engine instead. Links can go out of date — a page that worked last year may have moved. Searching for the site's name is the fallback every time.

A warning first — how to check a price before you pay

Prices change often. Prices below come from earlier research done in October 2026 and are NOT freshly verified here — check the site itself before paying anything. Free options are listed first on purpose: start free, pay only when you know what you need.

How to check a price, step by step:

  1. Open the site's own pricing page. The price on the site's own page is the only price that counts.
  2. Look for the price of the exact thing you want: one course, or one month. Monthly prices and one-time prices are different things — do not mix them up.
  3. Look for the date the page was updated, if it shows one. A page updated this month is fresher than a page updated a year ago.
  4. If the price is in a different money unit than rupees, check what it is in rupees before you decide.
  5. If the site offers a free trial or a free first lesson, take it before paying.

How old is too old? A price from last month is probably close. A price from a year ago is not. The prices in this lesson are from October 2026 — treat them as old, and check the site.

The map (free, start here)

roadmap.sh/devops — open this first

Link: https://roadmap.sh/devops

What it teaches: the whole DevOps path in one picture: Linux, Git, Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform, CI/CD, monitoring, in order. It is a diagram — a drawing of the road, with each topic as a stop.

Why open it: it answers "what do I learn next?" in one page. When you finish one topic, the map shows you the next one.

What it does NOT do: it teaches little by itself. Use it as a map, not a course. Clicking a topic shows a short description and links to guides — the learning happens in those guides, not on the map.

Where to start on the page: the diagram is big, and that is normal. Start at the top left and follow it down in order: Linux first, then Git, then Docker. Do not jump to Kubernetes before Docker — the map's order is the learning order. Click the first topic you have not done yet, read its short description, and follow its links.

Free learning, topic by topic

Open these when you are on that topic. Each one says what it teaches and why to open it, before the link.

freeCodeCamp — free written guides and video series

Link: https://freecodecamp.org

What it teaches: free written guides and video series on Linux, Docker, and Kubernetes basics. A written guide is text you read at your own speed. A video series is a set of videos in order.

Why open it: free, beginner-friendly, and easy to follow in small pieces. You can do one short guide at a time.

How to find the right guide: the site is big. Do not wander. Use the search box on the site and type the topic you are on, for example "Docker basics" or "Linux commands". Open the guide that matches your topic and your level (beginner). If the first guide is too hard, search again with the word "beginner" added.

TechWorld with Nana (YouTube) — slow explanations with diagrams

Link: https://youtube.com/@TechWorldwithNana

What it teaches: video explanations of Docker, Kubernetes, and CI/CD ideas, spoken slowly, with diagrams drawn on screen.

Why open it: pick this when a written guide is not clicking and you want a concept explained slowly with pictures. Some people learn better by watching than by reading. If you read the Docker guide twice and it still does not make sense, watch one of these videos.

Which video first: open the channel page and look for the video with your topic in its title — for example, a video called "Docker Tutorial for Beginners" when you are on Docker. Start with the beginner video for your topic, not the newest video. The newest video may be about an advanced topic.

Learn Git Branching — a browser game that teaches Git branching

Link: https://learngitbranching.js.org

What it teaches: Git branching, by doing. A branch is a side copy of your work, so you can try something without touching the main copy. This site is a game in your browser: it shows you a picture of branches, asks you to type Git commands, and shows you what happens.

Why open it: you learn by doing, and mistakes cost nothing here. Nothing you type affects your real files — it is all pretend, inside the game.

Are you ready for it? Yes, if you know the basic Git words: commit (one saved version), branch (a side copy), merge (combine two branches). If those words are new, read the Git section of the cheat sheets lesson first (phase2/cheat-sheets.md), then come here.

Free GitHub DevOps roadmap — 34 guided hands-on modules

Link: https://github.com/chanupen/devops-roadmap

What it teaches: 34 hands-on modules, from Linux to Kubernetes. A module is one lesson with exercises. "Hands-on" means you do it on a machine, not just read about it.

Why open it: it is free, practical, and its labs run on a single Linux machine, so there is no cloud bill.

Which module first: start at module 1 (Linux) and go in order, or jump to the module for the topic you are on now. Do not skip the Linux modules if you are new to the terminal — everything later assumes you can use it.

If the link fails: search for the repository name, chanupen/devops-roadmap, in a search engine. That exact name is what to type.

Docker's official getting-started guide — the toolmaker's own first steps

Link: https://docs.docker.com/get-started

What it teaches: Docker's own first steps, in order: what a container is, how to run one, how to build an image.

Why open it: free, and it matches the current version — because the people who make Docker write it, they update it when Docker changes.

When to open it: when you start Docker, before any paid Docker course. Read it alongside Portfolio Project 1: the guide explains the ideas, the project makes you do them.

Kubernetes official tutorials — short guided exercises from the makers

Link: https://kubernetes.io/docs/tutorials

What they teach: short guided exercises for Kubernetes, written by the people who make Kubernetes. A tutorial here is one small exercise: do this, see that.

Why open them: free, and they match the current version.

When to open them: when you reach Kubernetes — after Docker makes sense. Do not open them first; Kubernetes assumes you already understand containers.

Pro Git book (free online) — the full story of Git

Link: https://git-scm.com/book

What it teaches: the full story of Git, including branching and conflicts, in a free online book. A conflict is when two branches changed the same lines and Git stops to ask you which lines to keep.

Why open it: free, written clearly, and it is the standard Git book — the one most people point to.

When to open it: when Git "clicks" — when you can already make commits, branches, and merges without looking at notes, and you want the deeper story. How do you know Git has clicked? If you can explain git pull, git fetch, and a merge conflict in your own words (see the interview pack, phase2/interview-pack.md, questions 3 and 4), you are ready. If not, practise Git first, then read the book.

Killercoda browser scenarios — a free terminal in your browser

Link: https://killercoda.com

What it teaches: free practice scenarios for Linux, Docker, Kubernetes, or Git, in a terminal that runs in your browser. A scenario is one small exercise with a goal, like "start this container and check it is running".

Why open it: no setup at all. Use it when your own machine is busy, or when you want to practise without installing anything.

How to tell free from paid on the site: the site lists some scenarios as free and others as paid. Look for the word "free" on the scenario itself before you start. The free offering can change — check the site. If a scenario asks you to sign in or pay, it is not a free one. Pick another.

Paid, when you are ready

Pay only when you know what you need, and only for the one topic you are stuck on. A paid course does not finish itself — if you cannot finish free work, an expensive course will not finish it for you.

KodeKloud — hands-on labs inside the browser, especially for Kubernetes

Link: https://kodekloud.com

What it teaches: hands-on labs inside the browser, especially for Kubernetes. "Hands-on labs" means you practise on real lab machines in the browser, instead of only watching videos.

Why open it: you practise on real lab machines instead of only watching videos. Watching is not doing — this site makes you do.

When to open it: when you want guided Kubernetes practice and the free tutorials are not enough.

Price: earlier research priced it around ₹1,300/month — unverified here. Check the site before paying. That figure is from October 2026 research, so it may have changed. Look for what the price includes: labs, videos, or both.

Udemy — one cheap course on one topic, during a sale

Link: https://udemy.com

What it teaches: one course on one topic — for example, Docker for beginners. You pay once per course and keep it.

Why open it: you pay once per course and keep it. You do not pay every month. And you buy only the topic you need.

When to open it: during a sale, for one topic you are stuck on. Udemy runs sales often. Do not pay the full listed price — wait for a sale.

Price: earlier research found courses around ₹449–₹3,499 — unverified here. That range is very wide, so it is not a useful number by itself. Check the site before paying.

How to pick a good course (because quality varies by instructor):

  1. Check the rating. Look for 4.5 stars or higher, with many ratings (hundreds, not five). A high rating from five people means little.
  2. Check the last-updated date. Look for a course updated within the last year. A course updated three years ago may teach old steps that no longer work.
  3. Read a few recent reviews. Look for reviews that say "I finished it" and "the steps worked". Ignore reviews that only say "great course".
  4. Check the course length and the number of exercises. A course with exercises beats a course with only videos.

HashiCorp's own Terraform tutorials — free, from the makers of Terraform

Link: https://developer.hashicorp.com/terraform/tutorials

What they teach: Terraform, step by step, written by the people who make Terraform.

Why open them: free, written by the makers, and they match the current version.

When to open them: when you reach Terraform, before any paid Terraform course. Open the "Get Started" collection on the site and follow it in order — that collection is the first tutorial series for beginners.

Free learning for the data tier (Phase 1)

These are the toolmakers' own docs. Start with the lab in this kit first, then open the docs when you want the full story. Each one says which page to start on — do not open the docs' front page and wander.

Apache Kafka docs — topics, producers, consumers, MirrorMaker 2

Link: https://kafka.apache.org/documentation

What it teaches: the full story of Kafka: topics (named lines of messages), producers (senders), consumers (readers), and MirrorMaker 2 (the tool that copies a topic from one cluster to another).

Why open it: free, written by the people who make Kafka.

When to open it: when you finish the Kafka lab and want to understand it properly. Start on the documentation's front page and use its table of contents to find "topics" first — that is the idea everything else builds on.

PostgreSQL docs — replication, WAL, failover

Link: https://postgresql.org/docs

What it teaches: the full story of PostgreSQL, including replication (keeping a second copy up to date), WAL (the Write-Ahead Log — the primary's diary of every change), and failover (switching to the standby when the main database dies).

Why open it: free, and it is the standard PostgreSQL book.

When to open it: when you finish the PostgreSQL lab and want to understand replication, WAL, and failover properly. Start with the chapter called "High Availability" — that is the one to read. It covers the failover ideas in one place.

Patroni docs — leader election and automatic failover

Link: https://patroni.readthedocs.io

What it teaches: how Patroni works: leader election (how the watchers agree on which standby becomes the new leader) and automatic failover (promoting the standby by itself, within seconds).

Why open it: free, written by the people who make Patroni.

When to open it: when you finish the Patroni part and want to understand it properly. Start with the front page's introduction, then the section on how Patroni decides on a leader.

Redis docs — the full list of commands

Link: https://redis.io/docs

What it teaches: every Redis command, each with an example. The commands you used in the lab — SET, GET, SETEX, TTL, INCR, INFO — are all there, with more you have not met yet.

Why open it: free, and every command has an example. When you forget what a command does, this is the place to look.

When to open it: when you finish the Redis lab and want the full list. Use the search box on the site and type the command name — for example SETEX — to jump straight to it.

Moto docs — the S3 stand-in and the aws tool

Link: https://docs.getmoto.org

What it teaches: how Moto works: the pretend object store that acts like the real S3 store, and the aws tool you use to talk to it.

Why open it: free, written by the people who make Moto.

When to open it: when you finish the object-store lab and want to understand the S3 stand-in and the aws tool. Start with the front page's introduction, then the section on S3.

NGINX docs — upstreams, proxy settings, health checks

Link: https://nginx.org/en/docs

What it teaches: how NGINX works as a load balancer: upstreams (the list of backends behind the balancer), proxy settings (how the balancer passes requests on), and health checks (how the balancer knows a backend is dead).

Why open it: free, and it matches the current version.

When to open it: when you finish the load-balancer lab and want to understand it properly. Start with the front page and look for the load-balancing guide in the table of contents.

Which one do I open first for MY problem?

The list above is organised by topic. But a beginner usually has a specific stuck point, not a topic. Use this table to pick.

  • "I don't know what to learn next." → Open roadmap.sh/devops. Find where you are on the map. The next stop is your answer.
  • "I read the guide twice and it still doesn't make sense." → Open TechWorld with Nana on YouTube. Find the beginner video for your topic. Watch it slowly.
  • "I want to practise Git branching without breaking my real files." → Open Learn Git Branching (learngitbranching.js.org). Mistakes cost nothing there.
  • "My container starts and stops at once." → Do not open a site first. Open the cheat sheets lesson (phase2/cheat-sheets.md), Docker section, and the interview pack (phase2/interview-pack.md), question 7. Then, if you want the full story, open Docker's getting-started guide.
  • "My pipeline failed and I don't know why." → Open the failed run in the Actions tab, read the first error line, then open the interview pack, question 16. The error line names the one thing to fix.
  • "I want to understand Terraform before I install it." → Open HashiCorp's Terraform tutorials, "Get Started" collection.
  • "I want to practise Kubernetes but my machine is busy." → Open Killercoda. Pick a free scenario for Kubernetes.
  • "I finished a lab and want the full story." → Open that tool's official docs from the data-tier list above (Kafka, PostgreSQL, Patroni, Redis, Moto, NGINX). Start on the chapter named in that section.
  • "I am stuck on one topic and free guides are not enough." → That is the only time to consider paying. Open Udemy for one course on that one topic, during a sale, after checking the rating and the last-updated date. Or open KodeKloud for Kubernetes labs. Check the site before paying.

What NOT to buy first

A big bootcamp before you have finished the free work

A bootcamp is a big, expensive, intensive course — usually costing tens of thousands of rupees — that promises to teach you everything in a few weeks or months.

Do not buy one before you have finished the free map, the zero-cost lab, and one portfolio project. "Finished" means: you can point at the map and say what you learned, you completed the zero-cost lab, and you have one project on GitHub with a README you wrote.

Why: if you cannot finish free work, an expensive course will not finish it for you. The course does not do the work. You do.

Any course that promises a job or a salary

No course can promise that, whatever its page says. A job depends on the employer, the interview, and your projects — not on a course certificate.

How to spot the promise: look for words like "guaranteed placement", "guaranteed job", "100% placement", "salary of ₹X after this course", or "we will get you hired". Those words are the warning sign. A good course says what it teaches. A bad course says what it guarantees.

The simple rule

  1. Map first — roadmap.sh. See the whole road, in order.
  2. Free guides second — freeCodeCamp, TechWorld with Nana, the official docs. One topic at a time, in the map's order.
  3. One paid course only for the topic you are stuck on — Udemy during a sale, or KodeKloud for Kubernetes labs. Check the site before paying.
  4. Your own projects all the way through — the projects are the part employers can actually see.

The projects are the proof. The sites are the help. Do not mix them up.
